Choosing useful tools

Study resources that complement the reader.

The chronological reader is intentionally narrow: it organizes sayings and references. Other tools are better for translation comparison, word study, geography, historical background, and full-chapter reading. This page explains what each kind of resource is good for and what to look for before you buy or use one.

Start with the full biblical passage

The most important companion to a sayings collection is a complete Bible. The reader gives you the quoted words and reference; the Bible gives you the narrator, audience, setting, preceding question, and response. For serious study, read at least the paragraph around a saying and often the full chapter.

If you prefer the KJV wording used by this site, a KJV Bible keeps wording consistent. If your goal is interpretation rather than wording consistency, comparing several translations can help reveal where older English vocabulary or sentence structure may obscure the sense for a modern reader.

Study Bible

Best for: keeping cross-references, introductions, maps, and brief notes beside the biblical text.

Look for: clear separation between Scripture and editorial notes, useful cross-references, readable type, and an approach that does not crowd the biblical text.

Limitation: study notes reflect an editorial or theological perspective. Compare the note with the passage rather than treating the note as part of the text.

Concordance

Best for: finding where a word appears across the Bible and tracing repeated vocabulary.

Look for: an edition matched to the translation you use. A KJV concordance is especially useful for older phrases such as “take no thought,” which modern readers may not instinctively search.

Limitation: a word search is not the same as a concept search. One theme can be expressed through several different words.

Bible dictionary

Best for: names, places, customs, institutions, festivals, historical background, and concise definitions.

Look for: entries that distinguish historical information from interpretation and that point you back to Scripture references.

Limitation: short dictionary articles necessarily simplify debates. Use them to orient yourself, not to end a difficult question.

Bible atlas

Best for: understanding movement between Galilee, Judea, Jerusalem, Samaria, the Jordan region, and surrounding areas.

Look for: maps tied to specific periods and events rather than only a single modern map of the region.

Limitation: some ancient place identifications are debated, and a map can imply more geographic precision than the text provides.

Gospel harmony

Best for: comparing parallel events and seeing how a proposed chronology relates Matthew, Mark, Luke, and John.

Look for: visible source references, a method note, and enough separation that you can still tell which Gospel contributes each line.

Limitation: every harmony makes editorial sequencing decisions. Compare the harmony with the individual Gospel narratives.

Parallel Bible or synopsis

Best for: comparing multiple translations or placing parallel Gospel passages side by side.

Look for: a layout that makes differences easy to see without forcing them into a single combined paragraph.

Limitation: comparison can become an end in itself. Return to the narrative flow after studying the differences.

A free-first study workflow

You do not need to buy anything to use this site well. A complete Bible, the chronological reader, and careful note-taking are enough to begin. Add specialized tools only when a question actually requires them.

  1. Read the saying in the reader.Note the section and source reference.
  2. Read the full passage in your Bible.Identify speaker, audience, setting, and narrative response.
  3. Use a concordance only if vocabulary matters.Trace the word across multiple contexts rather than assuming one occurrence defines every use.
  4. Use a map only if geography matters.Ask whether movement, distance, region, or political setting affects the scene.
  5. Use a harmony only if sequence or parallels matter.Compare sources instead of treating a combined order as the text itself.

What this project itself uses

The chronological framework is based primarily on Arthur Hinds’ public-domain The Complete Sayings of Jesus (1927). The wording follows the public-domain King James Version tradition, with references and speaker boundaries cross-checked against the project’s structured source data.

That source choice reflects the project’s purpose: a stable, reusable English text and an established harmony framework. It does not imply that Hinds’ chronology is the only possible chronology or that the KJV is the only useful English translation.

How to choose a resource without wasting money

  • Buy a tool because it answers a question you actually have, not because a large reference shelf feels more serious.
  • Prefer resources that show their sources and separate biblical text from commentary.
  • For chronology, choose a harmony that explains its method and keeps Gospel references visible.
  • For word study, make sure the concordance or lexicon matches the translation and language level you are using.
  • For maps, prioritize readable historical maps over decorative graphics.
  • For study notes, compare viewpoints when an issue is contested rather than assuming one note settles it.
