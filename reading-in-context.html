Reading tools and method

How to read Jesus’ words in context

A quotation is a doorway into a passage. These examples show how to use the reader without treating a harmony excerpt as a self-contained transcript.

1. Start with the audience: “Judge not”

Matthew 7:1 is often quoted alone. Read through verse 5: the image of a beam and a speck turns the reader’s attention toward hypocrisy and self-examination. Verse 5 ends with seeing clearly enough to help someone else. Then notice the change of image in verse 6. Context does not erase the opening warning; it gives you more material for understanding what kind of judgment the passage is addressing.

In the sayings reader, search “judge” and open Matthew 7. Compare that setting with John 7:24, where Jesus is answering a dispute about an act of healing. Similar vocabulary does not mean identical circumstances.

2. Notice the speaker boundary: John 3

John 3:16 sits inside a chapter that begins with Nicodemus visiting Jesus. Read verses 1–15 before moving into verses 16–21. Who is asking questions? How do the images of birth, wind and the lifted-up serpent develop?

Ancient manuscripts do not supply modern quotation marks. Editions and interpreters differ about where Jesus’ direct speech ends and the narrator’s explanation begins. The historical harmony keeps a traditional extended speech. The chapter reader shows the selected translation’s wording in full, so you can see the transition rather than mistake red-letter boundaries for part of the original manuscript.

3. Read the question before the parable

Open Luke 10:25–37. Before the story of the Samaritan, a legal expert asks about inheriting eternal life and then asks who his neighbor is. After the story, Jesus asks which person became a neighbor to the injured man. The shift from defining a category of people to describing an action is visible only when the exchange stays together.

Read the priest, Levite and Samaritan as characters within the narrated comparison. Avoid turning a modern group into one of those characters without explaining the interpretive step. The site’s topic labels are routes into texts; they are not substitutes for the question and answer around them.

4. Compare accounts without making a new quotation

The Lord’s Prayer appears in Matthew 6:9–13 and Luke 11:2–4. Matthew places it within teaching about almsgiving, prayer and fasting. Luke introduces it with a disciple’s request to be taught to pray. Open both chapters and note their introductions, wording and length.

Switch between KJV and BSB at the same verse. Some differences reflect translation choices; others reflect the source text followed by an edition, including the prayer’s concluding doxology. Our chapter reader is not a critical apparatus and does not settle manuscript questions. The translation guide explains what comparison can show and where a study edition’s notes become useful.

A repeatable reading habit

  1. Find the saying and open its chapter.
  2. Read the paragraph before it and identify the audience.
  3. Read the response and the narrator’s explanation after it.
  4. Open another account when parallel context is offered.
  5. Separate the biblical wording from your own summary or application.
