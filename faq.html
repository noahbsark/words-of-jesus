Project questions

Frequently asked questions.

The title “Every Word of Jesus” sounds simple, but building a searchable chronology requires decisions about sources, speaker boundaries, parallel Gospels, and what counts as one displayed saying. These answers make those decisions explicit.

Every Word of Jesus editorial project · Updated September 4, 2026

What does “Every Word of Jesus” mean on this site?

It means the project attempts to gather statements directly attributed to Jesus in the New Testament source tradition used by the site, rather than presenting a hand-picked collection of favorite quotations. The focus is direct speech, organized for chronological reading and search.

The title does not mean that the site can recover words that were never recorded, nor that the English KJV wording is itself the language Jesus spoke historically. It means “the recorded sayings represented in this project’s New Testament source collection.”

Why does the reader show 486 displayed entries?

That number is an editorial count of displayed speaking turns across the full augmented collection. It is not a biblical or theological number. The Gospel ministry is organized into continuous speaking turns, and later New Testament material is displayed in separate sections.

A different editor could divide one long discourse into more entries or combine several short responses into fewer. The count is useful for navigation, saving, and sharing, but it should never be treated as though the manuscripts numbered the sayings this way.

Why use the King James Version?

The KJV provides a stable public-domain English text in the United States and is widely recognized by readers who search for traditional wording. Its public-domain status also allows the site to display the text without licensing restrictions that can apply to many modern translations.

The project does not claim that the KJV is the only legitimate translation. Readers studying interpretation, textual variants, or contemporary English wording should compare additional translations and, where appropriate, the underlying Greek text.

Is the chronological order definitive?

No. The chronology is a reading aid based primarily on Arthur Hinds’ 1927 harmony and additional editorial cross-checking. Many events can be placed with high confidence relative to major narrative stages, while some smaller sequencing questions admit more than one reasonable arrangement.

The site therefore distinguishes biblical text from editorial structure. Section titles, era labels, and event order are part of the study tool. For details, read How the chronology was built.

Why do similar sayings sometimes appear more than once?

Because similar wording does not always mean the Gospels are describing one historical occasion. Jesus may repeat teaching, and the evangelists can place comparable sayings in distinct narrative settings. Automatically deduplicating every verbal parallel would risk erasing repeated teaching.

When multiple Gospels clearly describe the same event, the harmony can align the accounts. When the setting is different or the relationship is uncertain, keeping separate entries can be the more transparent choice.

Why are some red-letter boundaries disputed?

Ancient manuscripts do not use red ink to mark Jesus’ speech. Red-letter editions are editorial. In most narrative scenes the speaker is obvious, but a few passages transition from dialogue to theological narration without a modern quotation mark telling every editor exactly where the change occurs.

John 3:16–21 is the best-known example. Some editions continue Jesus’ speech; others understand the evangelist’s explanatory voice to begin earlier. Revelation 1:8 presents a different attribution question involving divine titles. This site follows a traditional attribution in such cases while acknowledging the uncertainty.

Does the site include sayings after the Ascension?

Yes, when the New Testament narrative explicitly presents Jesus as speaking or reports a saying attributed to the Lord. Those items are separated from the Gospel-ministry chronology so the different literary setting is visible.

This is why the site has later sections rather than simply ending at the close of the four Gospels. The separation prevents a heavenly saying in Revelation, for example, from appearing as though it belonged to the same narrative stage as Galilean ministry.

Why not include every Old Testament statement spoken by “the LORD”?

Because identifying every Old Testament speech of the LORD specifically as a saying of Jesus requires a theological inference beyond the direct narrative attribution used by this project. Christians can make theological arguments about divine identity and Christology, but the collection uses a narrower editorial rule for inclusion.

That narrower rule keeps the project’s claim testable: the New Testament text itself attributes the speech to Jesus, or a New Testament narrator explicitly reports it as his saying.

How are the topic guides created?

A topic guide starts with the chronological sayings collection, searches for relevant vocabulary and scenes, then groups representative passages around a focused study question. The explanatory text is original editorial synthesis; Scripture quotations remain visually distinct and retain their references.

Software-assisted tools are used during drafting and site production. Quoted wording, references, and reader links are cross-checked against the project’s KJV collection before publication. The aim is to reduce search friction and surface relationships worth studying, not to manufacture pages around unrelated keywords.

Do the topic pages contain every relevant verse?

No. They are curated guides, not exhaustive concordances. A modern topic word can be narrower or broader than the biblical vocabulary. For example, a guide to “worry and anxiety” must account for KJV phrases such as “take no thought,” while a guide to “money” can involve treasure, riches, mammon, covetousness, stewardship, and generosity.

The chronological reader remains the better tool when you want to explore every occurrence of a word or phrase. Topic pages are better when you want a structured starting point.

Does this site replace reading the Bible in context?

No. The opposite is the design goal. Search should help you find a passage faster, then the reference should send you back into the full Gospel narrative. The site intentionally keeps references, section titles, and chronology visible because isolated quotations are easier to misunderstand.

For a practical context-first workflow, see How to study the words of Jesus with this reader.

Is this a denominational commentary?

No denomination is presented as the official interpretive authority of the site. The editorial focus is narrower: source organization, chronology, speaker attribution, reference checking, and thematic navigation.

Topic summaries inevitably use interpretive language, but they aim to distinguish summary from quotation and to avoid presenting debated chronology or red-letter boundaries as unquestionable manuscript facts.

Can I copy or quote the KJV text from the site?

The KJV text used here is treated as public domain in the United States. The site’s original explanatory writing, page design, and compiled editorial organization are separate from the underlying public-domain Bible wording.

If you are publishing Scripture for an audience outside the United States, check the applicable rights situation in your jurisdiction rather than assuming every territory treats the KJV identically.

How should I cite this project?

If you are quoting Scripture, cite the biblical reference first. If you are referring to the site’s chronology, say that you are using the Every Word of Jesus chronological arrangement and link to the relevant page or to the chronology method.

This keeps the distinction clear between the source text and the project’s editorial organization.

How can I report an error?

Use the Contact page to report a wording, reference, chronology, broken-link, or display issue. The most useful report includes the saying number or page URL, the reference in question, and a short description of what appears wrong.

Corrections to source text and references are more important than preserving an earlier editorial choice. The project is intended to be auditable and correctable.
