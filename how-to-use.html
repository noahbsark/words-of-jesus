Practical study guide

How to study the words of Jesus without losing the context.

A searchable quotation collection is convenient, but convenience can encourage shallow reading. This guide shows how to use search, chronology, parallel references, and topic pages as starting points while keeping the Gospel narrative in view.

Every Word of Jesus editorial project · Updated September 4, 2026

Start with a question, not a slogan

The reader works best when you begin with a real study question. “What does Jesus say about prayer?” is more useful than looking for one sentence to confirm an answer you already chose. A question gives you permission to notice variety: commands, warnings, parables, promises, examples, and sayings addressed to different audiences.

Use the search box for a word or phrase, but remember that English vocabulary is only one doorway into a theme. A search for forgive will not automatically find every passage about mercy, reconciliation, debt, pardon, repentance, or refusing retaliation. That is why the site pairs raw search with curated topic guides.

A five-part workflow

  1. Search broadly.Try the obvious word first, then related words. If you are studying prayer, search “pray,” “ask,” “Father,” “watch,” and “believe.” Keep a short list of passages that appear repeatedly important.
  2. Locate the saying in the timeline.Notice whether it belongs to early ministry, Galilean teaching, the journey to Jerusalem, the final week, the Last Supper, the Passion, or a later New Testament setting. Position in the story often changes how a saying sounds.
  3. Read the source reference.Open the Bible and read before and after the quoted words. Identify the audience, the question or event that prompted the saying, and the narrative response. Do not let the excerpt substitute for the chapter.
  4. Compare parallel accounts.If more than one Gospel records the scene, read each account on its own before combining them. Ask what each evangelist emphasizes and whether the wording, audience, or surrounding narrative differs.
  5. Summarize in your own words.Write one or two sentences describing what the saying contributes in its setting. If your summary cannot account for the context you just read, revise it.

Six questions to ask of any saying

Who is being addressed?

A crowd, the Twelve, opponents, one individual, a grieving family, or disciples in private conversation can receive very different kinds of instruction.

What prompted the words?

Look for the question, accusation, miracle, parable, meal, journey, or crisis immediately surrounding the statement.

What kind of speech is it?

Command, promise, warning, proverb, parable, prayer, symbolic language, legal dispute, or prediction should not all be read in exactly the same way.

Is there a parallel Gospel account?

A parallel can confirm the shared core of an event while also showing what one writer chooses to emphasize.

What happens next?

The response of listeners, disciples, opponents, or the narrator can clarify the force of the saying.

Am I importing a modern category?

Topics such as “anxiety,” “leadership,” or “finances” can be useful study labels, but they are modern umbrellas. Let the passage define its own terms before applying yours.

A twenty-minute study session

You do not need a complicated system. A focused twenty minutes can be enough to move from a keyword to responsible context.

Minutes 1–3: Search one theme and select two or three sayings that appear central rather than collecting every possible result.
Minutes 4–8: Read the Gospel paragraph around the first saying. Note the speaker, audience, location if given, and what caused the conversation.
Minutes 9–12: Check a parallel account if the reference suggests one. List one similarity and one difference in emphasis.
Minutes 13–16: Repeat the context check for a second saying from a different part of the ministry.
Minutes 17–20: Write a short synthesis: what is repeated, what changes with context, and what question remains open?

Use topic guides as maps, not final answers

The topic guides are curated pathways through representative sayings. They are deliberately selective. A page about love, for example, brings together love of God, neighbor, enemy, and fellow disciples. A page about money and wealth groups treasure, covetousness, generosity, mammon, stewardship, and riches. The guide makes those relationships easier to notice, but the Gospel references remain the evidence.

When a guide says that several passages share a theme, test the claim yourself. Follow the reader links, compare the narrative settings, and decide whether the summary accurately represents the text. The site is designed to make that checking easy.

Three common mistakes this reader cannot prevent for you

1. Proof-texting

Search makes it easy to find a sentence and hard to remember the paragraph. A familiar line can be part of a dispute, a parable, or an answer to a specific person. The cure is simple: read around the saying every time it becomes important to your conclusion.

2. Treating chronology as certainty

A harmony necessarily makes sequencing decisions. Those decisions can be reasonable without being provable in every case. Use chronological proximity as a study clue, not as an argument that depends on exact day-by-day precision unless the Gospel itself supplies that precision.

3. Treating red letters as manuscript markup

Red-letter boundaries are editorial. Most sayings are clear, but a small number of passages have disputed speaker transitions. If an argument depends on whether a particular sentence is direct speech from Jesus or narration by an evangelist, consult the passage and note the uncertainty.

For teachers, writers, and group leaders

If you quote material discovered here, cite the biblical reference as your primary source. The site can be cited for its chronology or editorial organization, but the KJV passage itself should remain the authority for the wording. When a lesson depends on a harmony choice, tell your audience that it is a harmony choice. When a speaker boundary is debated, say so.

This kind of transparency usually improves teaching rather than weakening it. It helps listeners distinguish what the text explicitly says from how a study tool organizes the text.
