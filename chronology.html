Editorial method

How this chronology was built.

A chronology of Jesus’ recorded sayings is useful only if its editorial choices are visible. This page explains what “chronological” means on this site, where the ordering comes from, how parallel Gospel material is handled, and which decisions should be treated as reading aids rather than settled historical facts.

1. What “chronological” means here

The word chronological can suggest a precision that the surviving texts do not always provide. The Gospels tell a historical story, but they do not supply a modern date stamp for every episode, and the evangelists sometimes arrange material in ways that emphasize theme, geography, conflict, or theological development. A harmony therefore involves editorial work: deciding when two accounts describe the same event, deciding which sequence is most useful for continuous reading, and deciding when similar sayings should remain separate because they may have been spoken on different occasions.

On this site, chronological means a reasoned reading order, not a claim that every item can be assigned to a provable day and hour. The goal is to help a reader move through the ministry as a developing narrative: preparation, early ministry, Galilean teaching, journeys, the final week, the Last Supper, the Passion, resurrection appearances, and later New Testament sayings attributed to Jesus.

This distinction matters because the order itself is not the biblical text. The KJV wording and Scripture references are source material; the section titles, entry numbers, era labels, and the decision to place one event before another are editorial scaffolding. They are useful scaffolding, but they should never be confused with inspired chapter-and-verse content.

2. Source base

The primary chronological framework is Arthur Hinds’ The Complete Sayings of Jesus (1927), a public-domain Gospel harmony built to gather and order the sayings of Jesus. Hinds provides the backbone for the Gospel sequence. The wording on this site follows the public-domain King James Version tradition rather than reproducing Hinds’ typography as a separate textual edition.

References and red-letter boundaries were cross-checked against a complete KJV source and structured red-letter data. That cross-check is important because a printed harmony can contain typographical conventions, merged references, or editorial speaker decisions that need to be separated from the underlying verse references.

No single harmony is treated as a universally binding chronology. Different harmonies can place some events differently, especially when the Gospel writers do not explicitly connect two scenes with an exact time marker. Where that kind of uncertainty affects interpretation, this project favors transparency over false precision.

3. How sayings become entries

The reader is organized around continuous speaking turns. An entry is not intended to equal one verse. A single speaking turn may cross several verses; another verse may contain a short saying within a larger narrated event. This is why the site’s total number of displayed entries is an editorial count, not a canonical number.

The practical rule is to keep words together when the narrative presents them as one continuous address and to split them when the speaker, audience, scene, or narrative action clearly changes. This makes the reader easier to search and save without pretending that the Bible itself numbers “Saying 1,” “Saying 2,” and so on.

Narrator text is generally not converted into a saying merely because it explains what Jesus did or meant. The focus is direct speech attributed to Jesus in the New Testament. Likewise, Old Testament speeches of “the LORD” are not automatically imported into the collection as sayings of Jesus, because doing so would require a theological identification beyond explicit narrative attribution.

4. Parallel Gospel accounts are connected, not erased

Matthew, Mark, and Luke contain substantial overlapping material, while John often supplies different scenes, different wording, or extended discourses not found in the Synoptics. A harmony is tempted to flatten those accounts into one seamless transcript. This project tries not to do that.

When multiple Gospels describe the same event, the reader can place those records together in the chronology while still preserving the references that show where each witness comes from. The point is not to invent a fifth Gospel. The point is to make the relationships among the four easier to study.

Similar wording does not automatically prove that two passages record the same occasion. Jesus could repeat teachings. Conversely, different wording does not automatically prove different events; ancient narrative can summarize the same event differently. Harmony decisions therefore rely on more than matching phrases: setting, audience, preceding and following events, geography, and narrative purpose all matter.

For a fuller explanation of this problem, see What a Gospel harmony can and cannot do.

5. The eleven reading eras

The site groups the full collection into eleven broad eras. These labels are navigation aids rather than biblical headings.

Childhood & Preparation

Material leading into the public ministry: early narrative context, preparation, baptism-related scenes, and the transition toward public teaching.

Early Ministry

Initial disciples, early Judean and Galilean activity, and the first extended encounters that establish major themes of identity, belief, and new life.

Galilean Ministry

A large body of teaching and healing activity in Galilee, including calls to discipleship, conflict over authority, and instruction to crowds and followers.

Teachings & Miracles

Parables, signs, disputes, private explanations, and public teaching that deepen the themes of faith, the kingdom, mercy, judgment, and discipleship.

Journey to Jerusalem

Teachings associated with movement toward Jerusalem and with the growing cost of discipleship, including warnings, table fellowship, prayer, possessions, and readiness.

Final Week

Jerusalem controversies, temple teaching, parables of accountability, questions from opponents, and teaching about watchfulness and judgment.

Last Supper

The final meal and farewell discourse: service, betrayal, love, abiding, the Spirit, prayer, and preparation for the disciples’ life after Jesus’ departure.

Passion & Crucifixion

Words spoken during arrest, trial, suffering, and crucifixion, kept close to the narrative setting in which the evangelists record them.

Resurrection & Ascension

Post-resurrection appearances, commissions, restoration, instruction, and sayings leading to the close of the Gospel narratives.

Post-Ascension

New Testament scenes outside the four Gospels in which the narrative explicitly presents Jesus as speaking, such as appearances or quoted heavenly speech.

Revelation & Hebrews

Later sayings presented separately so they are not silently blended into the Gospel-ministry chronology. This category makes the change of literary setting visible.

6. Hard cases and disputed speaker boundaries

Red-letter printing is an editorial convention. Ancient manuscripts do not contain red ink identifying where Jesus’ speech begins and ends. Usually the narrative makes the speaker obvious, but some passages leave room for disagreement.

John 3:16–21 is a familiar example. Some editions continue Jesus’ speech through those verses; other interpreters understand the evangelist’s explanatory voice to begin earlier. Revelation 1:8 raises a different attribution question because several divine titles appear in close succession. The project follows a traditional red-letter approach in such cases while explicitly acknowledging that the boundary is interpretive.

Another hard case is duplication. A memorable saying may appear in more than one Gospel or in more than one scene. The site does not assume that every verbal parallel should collapse into one entry. If the narratives place a saying in distinct settings, keeping both can be more faithful to the source presentation than forcing a single occurrence.

7. Why post-Ascension sayings are separated

The phrase “words of Jesus” can mean more than “red letters in Matthew through John.” Acts contains appearances and reported speech; Revelation contains extended heavenly speech; other New Testament books can quote sayings attributed to the Lord. Those texts belong to a different literary and chronological setting from the earthly ministry.

Rather than mixing them into the Gospel sequence as though all material came from one continuous biography, this site places them in later sections. That preserves the breadth of the New Testament witness while keeping the Gospel chronology intelligible.

8. How to use the chronology responsibly

A harmony is most useful when it sends you back to the Gospels rather than replacing them. If an entry is important to your study, read the chapter around it. If several references are listed, compare them. Notice who is being addressed, what question or conflict prompted the saying, and what happens next. A line that sounds universal when isolated may be part of a parable, a dispute, a private instruction to disciples, or a response to a specific person.

The chronology is therefore best treated as a map. A map helps you see sequence and relationships, but it is not the terrain itself. The Gospel narratives remain the primary context. The reader’s job is to reduce friction between discovery and context, not to remove context.

A practical check:

When you find a saying through search, read at least the surrounding paragraph in the Gospel, check any parallel references shown, and note whether the saying is public teaching, private instruction, a parable, a controversy, or a post-resurrection statement. That simple habit prevents many common proof-texting errors.
