Reading tools and method

Choose a Bible translation

Choose a translation you can understand, then use a second edition to investigate wording that deserves a closer look. Four complete 66-book editions are available in the chapter reader.

The four editions

King James Version (KJV, 1769 tradition)
Traditional English wording with “thee,” “thou” and older vocabulary. The historical harmony excerpts are based on this tradition, but can combine accounts or omit narration. The complete chapter reader is a separate text source.
Berean Standard Bible (BSB)
A contemporary English translation. This is a useful starting point when older English makes the KJV difficult to follow. The publisher dedicated the Berean text to the public domain in 2023.
American Standard Version (ASV, 1901)
An older, relatively formal translation. Its vocabulary and sentence structure can be less familiar than modern English. In the Old Testament it often uses “Jehovah” where other editions print “LORD.”
Young’s Literal Translation (YLT, 1898)
A historical translation that often retains unusual word order and verbal constructions. Its English can feel awkward. “Literal” in the title does not make every wording uniquely authoritative or remove the need for interpretation.

How the selector works

On the sayings page, “Bible context translation” changes where the context links take you. It does not silently rewrite a combined historical quotation into a modern version. In the Bible reader, switching translation keeps the same book, chapter and starting verse when that verse exists. The book and chapter controls let you keep reading through either testament.

Why verse numbers sometimes differ

Translations may follow different manuscript readings, and some verse numbers are omitted or placed in footnotes in modern editions. Compare John 5 around verses 3–5 in KJV and BSB. The surrounding chapter remains readable even when a numbered verse is absent. These plain-text files do not include a study Bible’s textual footnotes, maps or commentary. For a textual question, consult an edition with its translators’ notes.

What the reader does not claim

We do not rank editions by theological correctness. A difference in wording does not by itself establish what a Greek or Hebrew expression must mean. Neither does a historical harmony establish the precise words spoken in the original language. Use the supplied English texts as translations and return to their full narrative context.
